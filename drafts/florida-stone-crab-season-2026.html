Season Guide · Gulf Backcountry

Florida stone crab season is the rare harvest that doesn't kill anything. You take a claw, the crab goes back in the water, and it grows a new one. That is the whole appeal — and it is also why the rules are fussier than most people expect. Here is the 2026–27 season, the claw limits that actually get people cited, and how to set traps without wasting a year learning the hard way.

When is stone crab season in Florida?

The recreational stone crab season runs October 15 through May 1, and the fishery closes on May 2. That is a long window compared with lobster mini-season or scallops, and there is no separate two-day sport season — opening day is the same for everyone.

Note the "whichever is less" on the bag limit. Four people on one boat do not get four gallons. The vessel caps out at two, full stop, and that catches out more parties than any other stone crab rule.

The claw rules that actually get people cited

Measure the forearm, not the whole claw

The 2 7/8-inch minimum is the forearm — the immovable section, measured from the elbow to the first joint. It is not the tip-to-tip length, and eyeballing it is how undersized claws end up in the bucket. Measure every claw before it goes in the cooler, not after.

Claws only — never the whole crab

You cannot keep a whole stone crab, and you cannot transport one. The claw comes off at the boat or the trap and the crab goes straight back in the water. Possessing whole crabs is a separate violation from an undersized claw.

Check for a sponge before you take anything

An egg-bearing female carries an orange or brown spongy mass under her abdomen. If you see one, she goes back immediately and untouched — no claw. Flip every crab and look before you commit.

One claw or two? What the science says

This is the question every stone crabber argues about, so let's be precise: FWC does not explicitly prohibit taking both claws from a legal crab. But the agency's own research is blunt about the cost. In a published study cited in FWC's stone crab FAQ, 47 percent of crabs declawed by double amputation died from the trauma, against 28 percent for a single amputation.

Taking one claw roughly halves the kill rate on a fishery whose entire premise is that the animal survives. We take one and put her back. It is not a legal requirement; it is the difference between a renewable harvest and a slow-motion cull.

Break it right or the claw never grows back

A stone crab regrows a claw only if the joint connecting it to the body stays intact. Done correctly the break happens at a natural fracture plane and a membrane seals the wound, which stops the bleeding. Twist or tear the claw and you rip out the joint, the crab bleeds, and nothing grows back. Support the body, hold the claw at the base, and apply steady pressure until it releases cleanly.

How to trap stone crabs recreationally

Traps are the practical way to do this — stone crabs live in burrows and you will not reliably catch them any other way.

  • Five traps per person, maximum. That is the recreational cap and it is per person, not per vessel.
  • Register them first. Everyone 16 and older needs the free recreational stone and/or blue crab trap registration, done online at GoOutdoorsFlorida.com — even if you are normally exempt from a fishing licence. Stone crab registration numbers start with the letter S, and each trap must permanently display your name, address and that number.
  • Check the trap itself is legal. Since the 2024-25 season every recreational stone crab trap needs an unobstructed 2 3/16-inch escape ring in a vertical exterior wall, plus the degradable panel and a buoy at least 6 inches marked with a legible 2-inch R. A trap bought second-hand may predate the ring rule.
  • Pull by hand, in daylight only. No mechanical trap pullers on recreational gear, and no pulling before sunrise or after sunset.
  • Set them on hard or mixed bottom in 4–20 feet. Stone crabs like structure — rock, rubble, oyster, seagrass edges — near passes and channel edges rather than open sand.
  • Bait with something oily. Pig's feet, cow bones and oily fish carcasses are the traditional picks because they last several days in a soak.
  • Give it a proper soak. Two to five days beats checking daily. Every pull disturbs the set.

Traps may go in the water 10 days before the season opens — October 5 — so your gear is soaking and ready on opening morning. One catch that trips people up: you may not tend those traps until the season actually starts. Placing them early is legal; pulling them early is not.

Where to find stone crabs in Florida

The southwest Gulf coast is the heart of the fishery. Everglades and Florida Bay and the Charlotte Harbor backcountry produce the bulk of Florida's stone crab, with Naples, Marco Island and the Ten Thousand Islands the classic grounds. The Keys produce well, and there is a real fishery on the Atlantic side from the Space Coast south, just at lower density.

Look for hard bottom adjacent to deeper water — the edge of a channel, a rubble patch off a pass, the drop off a grass flat. Muddy open bottom holds far fewer crabs than most people assume.

Gear you actually need

This is a short list, which is part of the appeal.

  • Registered traps with your number on them, plus buoys and line rated for your depth. A standard plastic or wire stone crab trap already has the escape ring and degradable panel — check before you buy second-hand.
  • A claw gauge — the single item that keeps you legal. Measure every claw.
  • Heavy gloves. A stone crab claw generates crushing force measured in hundreds of pounds. This is not a place to improvise; cut-resistant gloves are the minimum.
  • A cooler with ice. Claws are best cooked the day they are caught. A floating cooler works if you are wading or working from a small boat.
  • A mallet or crab cracker for the kitchen end of the job — claws are cracked, never twisted apart.

Cooking your claws

Cook them the day you catch them. Raw claw meat sticks to the shell and turns to mush, which is why almost every stone crab claw you have ever eaten was cooked before it was chilled.

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a hard boil.
  2. Drop the claws in and boil 8 minutes for large claws, 6 for smaller ones.
  3. Move them straight into an ice bath for a few minutes to stop the cooking and release the meat from the shell.
  4. Chill, then crack with a mallet — tap the shell rather than crushing it, so you get clean meat instead of shell fragments.

Serve cold with mustard sauce. That is the traditional treatment and it is traditional because it is right. For handling the rest of what you bring home, our guide to cleaning and cooking your catch covers the finfish side.

Bottom line

Bottom line: Stone crab opens October 15 and runs to May 1. Five registered traps per person, hand-pulled in daylight, on hard bottom in 4–20 feet. Claws only, 2 7/8-inch forearm minimum, one gallon per person or two per vessel whichever is less, and nothing at all from a sponged female. Take one claw, break it clean at the joint, and she grows it back — which is the entire point of this fishery.

Fishing the same water

The backcountry that produces stone crab is the same water that holds snook, redfish and tarpon, and a lot of people run traps on the way to fish. If you want to learn those grounds, a local guide is the fastest route in.
